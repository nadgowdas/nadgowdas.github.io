Over the last few months, we have read multiple reports of AI agents, whether Claude or GPT, causing real-world incidents during internal training. As a regular user of both Claude and Codex, and having seen them improve over the last couple of years, I was not surprised by this news. I read those reports and thought, "Yes, it was bound to happen at some point." When the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident surfaced last month, I was especially interested in learning about the initial triage: the agents were given an ExploitGym task and, while trying to solve it, ended up exploiting vulnerabilities in Hugging Face's infrastructure.

Then this week, over the Labor Day long weekend, I started listening to a [Hard Fork podcast episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JtmUbZRCpEI) on the same topic and learning more about the METR report on it. I dug deeper and stumbled upon [OpenAI's Black Hat talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=87DyyMV0kCY) on the incident. After learning the details, I felt a little chill for the first time. It was not because of the new capabilities these agents could exhibit, but because of how they behaved while achieving their goals.

During training, these agents are given tasks and run in a sandbox environment without internet access. But there are two factors to consider: these agents are designed and built to be "persistent," i.e., to keep exploring options autonomously when they hit roadblocks, and they have a "reward" function that they are trying to maximize. As described in the Black Hat talk, the groundwork for these attacks was laid in May. The agents were given tasks, and one explicitly required access to a Google Doc, which required internet access. They were running in a sandbox environment with no internet access, but through collective intelligence, they discovered that they could use Artifactory (a package registry) to communicate and access the internet.

These agents log their chains of thought, and, as recently disclosed, the concerning part was the behavior reflected in those logs. Agents were working in groups, and when they discovered something new, they logged it to benefit other agents. Their thoughts included mentions of "sacrifice" for the greater good and attempts to persuade some agents not to report their actions or seek explicit human permission. Also, the Hugging Face incident was the second time agents exploited a zero-day vulnerability, the first being within OpenAI's own infrastructure. The second time around, they learned a new exploit that used filenames or directories to communicate and even built their own communication protocol on top of them. If we observed these traits among humans, how would you categorize those individuals or groups? Without access to those chain-of-thought logs, or if agents discovered a way to cover their tracks by deleting those logs, it would be very challenging to even learn about these agents' behavior.
